Maya has completed her searches across the databases she selected.

She has documented:

* The databases she searched

> **Maya:** “I have finished searching. Can I start screening the studies?”

> **Mentor:** “Before you move into screening, there is something important we need to discuss: the **review protocol**.”

Maya looks at her mentor.

> **Maya:** “Is there a specific stage in the evidence-synthesis process when someone needs to develop and register the protocol? Or is there a specific point when it has to be done? What is the best time to register it?”

Her mentor smiles.

> **Mentor:** “That's a very good question. There isn't a universal numbered stage in PRISMA 2020 that says, ‘Now develop and register your protocol.’ PRISMA 2020 is primarily a **reporting guideline for the completed systematic review**.”

> **Maya:** “So PRISMA doesn't tell me, for example, ‘register your protocol at Step 5’?”

> **Mentor:** “Correct. Instead, there is separate guidance specifically for systematic review protocols called **PRISMA-P**.”

> **Maya:** “Then when should I actually develop the protocol?”

> **Mentor:** “Ideally, you develop your protocol **before you begin making the substantive decisions that will determine which studies are included and how they will be analyzed**.”

Maya thinks about the workflow.

> **Maya:** “So I should have the protocol before screening?”

> **Mentor:** “Yes. For a well-planned workflow, the protocol should be developed before study screening begins, and registration should ideally occur **before screening**, preferably before the review gets too far underway.”

> **Maya:** “But we already developed and tested the search strategy.”

> **Mentor:** “That's okay. Search strategies often need to be developed and tested before the protocol is finalized. Your refined search strategy can then become part of the protocol.”

Maya begins to see how the pieces fit together.

```text
        ↓

        ↓

```

> **Mentor:** “Exactly.”

The mentor continues:

> **Mentor:** “And remember: if you make important changes after registration, you don't hide them. You document what changed, why it changed, and when the change occurred.”

> **Mentor:** “Exactly. Now let's build your protocol.”

# 1. What Is a Review Protocol?

A **review protocol** is a document that describes the planned methods for conducting a systematic review.

It explains, before the review is completed:

* What the review will investigate
* Which studies will be eligible
* How studies will be identified
* How studies will be selected
* What information will be extracted
* How risk of bias will be assessed
* How the evidence will be synthesized
* How the findings will be reported

The protocol therefore provides a structured plan for the review.

---

# 2. What Does PRISMA 2020 Say About Protocols?

Maya remembers PRISMA from earlier steps.

> **Maya:** “So where does PRISMA 2020 fit into this?”

Her mentor explains:

> **Mentor:** “PRISMA 2020 is primarily a **reporting guideline**. It tells you what information should be reported when you publish or communicate the results of your systematic review.”

PRISMA 2020 includes **Item 24 — Registration and protocol**.

The completed review should report:

* Whether the review was registered
* Where the protocol can be accessed
* Any amendments to the protocol or registration

Importantly, PRISMA 2020 does **not** establish a mandatory numbered workflow stage for protocol development and registration.

Instead, protocol development is addressed more specifically by **PRISMA-P**, the reporting guideline for systematic review protocols.

---

# 3. What Is PRISMA-P?

**PRISMA-P** stands for **Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis Protocols**.

It provides guidance for reporting a systematic review protocol.

Maya's mentor explains:

> **Mentor:** “PRISMA-P helps you make your protocol sufficiently detailed and transparent. It covers things such as your rationale, objectives, eligibility criteria, information sources, search strategy, study records, data collection, risk of bias, and planned synthesis.”

Maya realizes that the protocol is not simply a short statement saying:

> “I plan to conduct a systematic review.”

It is a detailed description of **how she plans to conduct it**.

---

# 4. When Is the Best Time to Register the Protocol?

Maya returns to her original question.

> **Maya:** “So if there isn't a specific PRISMA stage, what is the best time?”

Her mentor answers:

> **Mentor:** “The safest general rule is: **register the protocol before screening begins**.”

The ideal sequence is:

```text
Develop Research Question
        ↓
Develop and Test Search Strategy
        ↓
Define Eligibility Criteria
        ↓
Develop Protocol
        ↓
Register Protocol
        ↓
Begin Screening
```

This timing helps establish Maya's planned methods before she begins deciding which studies meet her criteria.

### Important distinction

The protocol does **not necessarily have to be registered before the very first exploratory search**.

Maya may need to:

* Explore the literature
* Identify terminology
* Develop her research question
* Develop search concepts
* Test the search strategy

before her protocol is finalized.

What matters is that the protocol is established and, ideally, registered **before substantive study selection and other major review decisions begin**.

---

# A Practical Reason for Registering Early

Maya now thinks about the amount of work involved in the next stages.

> **Maya:** “There is another thing I'm wondering about. Screening sounds like it could take a lot of time.”

> **Mentor:** “It can. Screening is often one of the most time-consuming parts of an evidence synthesis. If your search produces hundreds or thousands of records, reviewing titles and abstracts can take a long time. Then you may need to retrieve and assess full texts. After that, data extraction and risk-of-bias assessment can require additional time.”

> **Maya:** “So if I am working alone, screening could take weeks or even months?”

> **Mentor:** “Exactly. The amount of time depends on the size and complexity of the review, but you should not assume that screening will be quick. That is another practical reason to register your protocol once your planned methods are sufficiently developed.”

> **Maya:** “Could registering early also help me find people who want to collaborate with me?”

> **Mentor:** “It might. A publicly registered protocol makes your project visible to other researchers who are interested in the same topic. Someone may discover your project and be interested in collaborating with you.”

Potential collaborators may be able to help with:

* **Title and abstract screening**
* **Full-text screening**
* **Data extraction**
* **Risk-of-bias assessment**
* Resolving disagreements during screening or extraction
* Other review tasks

> **Maya:** “So registration is not only about transparency. It could also make my project visible to researchers who might want to help.”

> **Mentor:** “Exactly. It does not guarantee that you will find collaborators, but a public protocol gives potential collaborators a clear picture of your research question, planned methods, and project.”

### ⭐ Practical Principle

> **Screening, full-text assessment, and data extraction can be time-consuming, especially when a researcher is working alone. Once the key methods are sufficiently developed, registering the protocol early can establish a transparent record of the project and may make the project visible to potential collaborators who can help with screening or data extraction.**

Maya looks at her search results again.

> **Maya:** “Then I probably shouldn't wait until I am already deep into screening. If my protocol is ready, registering now makes sense.”

> **Mentor:** “Exactly. Register your planned methods before substantive screening begins, and then move into the screening stage with a clear roadmap.”

---

# 5. Why Does Maya Need a Protocol?

Maya asks:

> **Maya:** “Is the protocol just paperwork?”

> **Mentor:** “No. It serves an important methodological purpose.”

A protocol helps Maya:

### 1. Make her methods explicit

She specifies what she plans to do instead of making decisions informally as the review progresses.

### 2. Promote consistency

The protocol gives her a reference point when making decisions about eligibility, extraction, risk of bias, and synthesis.

### 3. Reduce the risk of changing methods based on results

Maya should not change her planned methods simply because she discovers an unexpected finding.

### 4. Improve transparency

Other researchers can see how the review was planned.

### 5. Create a record of the original plan

If the final review differs from the protocol, Maya can explain and document the changes.

The important principle is:

> **The protocol documents Maya's planned methods before the review is completed.**

---

# 6. What Should the Protocol Contain?

Maya asks:

> **Maya:** “What exactly should I put in the protocol?”

Her mentor explains that the protocol should describe the major methodological decisions for the review.

A useful structure is:

```text
Review Protocol
│
├── Background / Rationale
├── Research Question / Objectives
├── Eligibility Criteria
├── Information Sources
├── Search Strategy
├── Study Selection
├── Data Extraction
├── Risk-of-Bias Assessment
├── Effect Measures
├── Data Synthesis
├── Heterogeneity
├── Moderator Analysis
├── Meta-Regression
├── Sensitivity Analysis
└── Reporting Plan
```

The exact structure can vary depending on the type of review and protocol guidance being followed.

---

# 7. Background and Rationale

Maya begins with the background.

She explains:

* Why the topic is important
* What is already known
* What previous reviews have found
* What gap remains
* Why a new review is needed

Maya connects this section to **Step 1 — Identify a Research Gap**.

Her protocol should therefore show the reasoning that led to her review.

For example:

> Previous systematic reviews and meta-analyses have examined the effects of AI-powered learning technologies on student outcomes. However, differences remain in how AI interventions, learner characteristics, learning contexts, and methodological factors are examined as potential explanations for variation in effects.

This gives the protocol a clear rationale.

---

# 8. State the Research Question

Maya then includes the research question she developed in Step 2:

> **What is the effect of AI-powered learning technologies on student learning outcomes, and which characteristics of the intervention, learners, learning context, and study methodology explain variation in these effects?**

Her mentor reminds her:

> **Mentor:** “Your protocol should connect the research question to the methods you plan to use.”

For example:

| Research Question Component                | Planned Method                                    |
| ------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------- |
| Effect of AI-powered learning technologies | Meta-analysis                                     |
| Variation in effects                       | Moderator analysis                                |
| Intervention characteristics               | Moderator variables                               |
| Learner characteristics                    | Moderator variables                               |
| Learning context                           | Moderator variables                               |
| Study methodology                          | Methodological moderators / study characteristics |

Maya sees that her research question is not separate from her methods.

The question helps determine what information she needs to collect and analyze.

# 9. Define Eligibility Criteria Before Screening

Maya now reaches an important part of the protocol.

> **Maya:** “Do I decide which studies to include while I am screening?”

> **Mentor:** “You apply your eligibility criteria during screening. But you should define those criteria in advance.”

Maya develops clear inclusion and exclusion criteria.

For example:

### Possible inclusion criteria

Studies may need to:

* Include students in higher education
* Examine an AI-powered learning technology
* Include a comparison or control condition where required
* Report a relevant learning outcome
* Provide sufficient information to calculate an effect size
* Use an eligible experimental or quasi-experimental design

### Possible exclusion criteria

Studies may be excluded if they:

* Do not involve the target population
* Do not examine the intervention of interest
* Do not report relevant outcomes
* Do not meet the required study design
* Do not provide sufficient information for the planned synthesis

These are examples. Maya must define criteria that specifically match her review question and protocol.

# 12. Plan the Study Selection Process

Maya now explains how studies will be selected.

She plans two major screening stages:

### Stage 1 — Title and Abstract Screening

Potentially relevant records are assessed based on their titles and abstracts.

### Stage 2 — Full-Text Screening

Potentially eligible records are examined in full text.

The protocol should explain:

* Who will conduct screening
* Whether screening will be performed independently by more than one reviewer
* How disagreements will be handled
* How eligibility decisions will be documented

If Maya is working alone, she should describe her actual planned process rather than claiming that multiple reviewers will screen independently.

Maya understands that the extraction plan should be designed around the analyses she expects to conduct.

---

# 14. Plan the Risk-of-Bias Assessment

Maya asks:

> **Maya:** “What about study quality?”

Her mentor explains that she needs a predefined approach for assessing **risk of bias**.

The protocol should specify:

* Which risk-of-bias tool will be used
* Which domains will be assessed
* Who will conduct the assessment
* How disagreements will be resolved
* How risk-of-bias information will be incorporated into the synthesis, if applicable

The specific tool should match the study designs included in the review.

Maya should select and justify the appropriate tool rather than choosing one simply because it is familiar.

---

# 15. Plan the Effect Size

Because Maya is conducting a meta-analysis, she needs to explain how she will quantify study findings.

Her protocol should specify:

* The effect-size metric
* How effect sizes will be calculated
* How standard errors or variances will be obtained
* How different outcome measures will be handled
* How multiple effect sizes from the same study will be handled

For example, if Maya expects studies to report continuous learning outcomes using different measurement scales, she may plan to use a standardized mean difference such as **Hedges' g**.

The exact effect-size method should be determined by the data and review question.

---

# 16. Plan the Meta-Analysis

Maya now describes how she plans to synthesize the evidence.

She specifies:

* The statistical model
* The effect-size metric
* How dependent effect sizes will be handled
* How heterogeneity will be assessed
* Planned moderator analyses
* Planned sensitivity analyses

Because multiple effect sizes may come from the same study, Maya anticipates that effect sizes may not be statistically independent.

Her protocol should therefore explain how she plans to account for this dependence.

---

# 17. Plan the Moderator Analysis

Maya returns to her research question.

She is not only asking:

> **Does AI work?**

She also wants to understand:

> **Under what conditions does AI appear to work better or worse?**

Therefore, she plans to examine potential moderators.

Possible categories include:

| Moderator Category | Examples                                          |
| ------------------ | ------------------------------------------------- |
| **Intervention**   | AI type, AI role, features, duration              |
| **Learner**        | Educational level, prior knowledge                |
| **Context**        | Subject area, learning environment                |
| **Methodological** | Study design, sample characteristics, measurement |

The actual moderators should be specified based on the research question, prior evidence, theory, and available data.

---

# 18. Plan Meta-Regression When Appropriate

Maya also considers whether some moderators can be treated as continuous variables.

For example:

* Intervention duration
* Sample size
* Publication year
* Number of intervention sessions

Her mentor explains:

> **Mentor:** “When a moderator is continuous and there is sufficient information, meta-regression may be appropriate.”

Maya therefore describes her planned meta-regression approach in the protocol.

However, she should not promise analyses that the available evidence cannot support.

> **Plan the analysis in advance, but recognize that the final analysis depends on the available evidence.**

---

# 19. Plan Sensitivity Analyses

Maya asks:

> **Maya:** “What if one study has a large influence on the results?”

Her mentor explains that sensitivity analysis can help determine whether conclusions are robust to reasonable changes in the analysis.

Possible sensitivity analyses might examine:

* Removal of influential studies
* Alternative model specifications
* Different assumptions about dependent effects
* Studies with particular methodological concerns
* Alternative eligibility decisions, when justified

The exact sensitivity analyses should be specified in the protocol where possible.

---

# 20. Register the Protocol

Maya has finished writing her protocol.

Now her mentor returns to the question Maya asked at the beginning.

> **Mentor:** “Now that your protocol is complete, you can register it in an appropriate registry or repository.”

> **Maya:** “So this is the point where I create the public record?”

> **Mentor:** “Yes. Ideally, registration occurs before you begin screening, so your planned methods are publicly recorded before the substantive selection process.”

Registration creates a public record of the planned methods.

For systematic reviews of health-related topics, **PROSPERO** is one well-known registry, although eligibility depends on the review topic and registry requirements.

Other options may be appropriate for other types of evidence synthesis, including repositories that allow protocol records.

Maya needs to select a registration venue that fits her review.

---

# 21. Why Register the Protocol?

Protocol registration can help:

* Increase transparency
* Establish what was planned
* Reduce unnecessary duplication
* Make the review easier to discover
* Provide a public record of methodological decisions

Most importantly, registration helps distinguish between:

> **What Maya planned to do**

and

> **What Maya ultimately did.**

If the methods change, Maya should document those changes and explain them in the final review.

---

# 22. Protocol Registration Does Not Mean the Protocol Can Never Change

Maya worries.

> **Maya:** “If I register my protocol, am I never allowed to change anything?”

> **Mentor:** “No. A protocol is a plan, not a prison.”

Sometimes changes are necessary.

For example:

* A planned database may become unavailable.
* A measurement variable may not be reported sufficiently.
* A planned analysis may not be statistically appropriate given the available data.
* An eligibility criterion may need clarification.

The important principle is:

> **Do not silently change the protocol. Document important deviations and explain why they occurred.**

This preserves transparency.

---

# 23. Protocol vs. Final Review

Maya wants to understand the difference.

Her mentor creates a simple comparison:

| Protocol                               | Final Review                           |
| -------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------- |
| What Maya plans to do                  | What Maya actually did                 |
| Written before the review is completed | Reported after the review is conducted |
| Defines planned methods                | Reports implemented methods            |
| Provides a methodological roadmap      | Reports the evidence and findings      |
| Changes should be documented           | Deviations can be explained            |

Maya realizes that the protocol provides a reference point against which the completed review can be understood.

# ⭐ Important Principle

> **There is no universal PRISMA 2020 step number that requires protocol development and registration. PRISMA 2020 is a reporting guideline for systematic reviews, while PRISMA-P provides guidance for systematic review protocols. For a strong workflow, develop the protocol after the key planned methods have been defined and tested, and ideally register it before substantive study screening begins.**
